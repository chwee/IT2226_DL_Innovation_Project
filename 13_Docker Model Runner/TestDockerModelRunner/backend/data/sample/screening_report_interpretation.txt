[TOPIC]      Overall Risk: HIGH — what it means and what to do
[REPORT SECTION] Overall Risk classification

[URGENCY]    Book dentist within 1–2 weeks
[TRIGGERS]

  - Is High risk an emergency?
  - How soon do I need to see a dentist if my risk is High?
  - Will I need surgery if my risk is High?
  - Can High risk get better with treatment?

CONTENT:

WHAT THE ORAL LINK ENGINE SAYS:
"Photos classified under High Risk show significant dental issues requiring immediate attention. This includes advanced decay, severe gum disease or substantial tooth damage. Immediate treatment is crucial to mitigate pain, prevent further damage, or infections, and restore oral health. A comprehensive dental examination is recommended to determine the specific treatments needed. Delay in addressing these concerns can lead to more severe health issues and complications."

WHAT IT MEANS IN PLAIN LANGUAGE:
A High overall risk means the screening has identified findings that warrant prompt dental attention. This is the most urgent of the three risk classifications. It typically reflects one or more of the following: an Advanced Cavity on a specific tooth (substantial decay unlikely to be managed with a simple filling); Advanced gum recession with significant root exposure; multiple missing teeth affecting chewing function or bite stability; heavy plaque or calculus across many teeth; or a combination of these.

A High risk is not necessarily a medical emergency — but it is a clear signal that the conditions identified are unlikely to resolve without professional treatment, and that delaying makes matters worse.

WHAT TO DO NEXT:
1. Book a dental appointment within the next one to two weeks — not a routine recall but a visit specifically to assess the screening findings.
2. Review the Potential Actions table on your report. High-priority items listed there are the ones to discuss first with the dentist.
3. Expect the visit to take longer than a routine check-up. The dentist may take X-rays, do a full periodontal assessment, and outline a treatment plan that unfolds across several appointments.
4. Be prepared for a possible specialist referral. Depending on specific findings — particularly Advanced gum recession or extensive decay — the dentist may refer to a periodontist, prosthodontist, or other specialist.
5. Bring your screening report and Potential Actions list to the appointment.

IN A DENTAL EMERGENCY:
If you are currently experiencing severe pain, facial swelling that affects breathing or swallowing, uncontrolled mouth bleeding, or facial trauma — do not wait. Go to the nearest A&E department or call 995. The screening report is not a substitute for emergency care.

WHAT HIGH RISK DOES NOT MEAN:
It does not mean you will lose teeth, need major surgery, or that all recommended treatments must happen at once. Most High-risk situations stabilise well once the most urgent items are addressed.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A Moderate overall risk means the AI detected one or more dental concerns that are no longer at the earliest stage. There may be Moderate-severity findings — visible plaque accumulation, gum recession with some root exposure, or other conditions that have progressed past the early-warning stage — or a combination of multiple Initial-stage findings that together suggest active attention is needed.

Moderate risk is not an emergency, but it is a clear signal that home care alone is not keeping up. The underlying causes need to be examined and managed in partnership with a dental professional.

WHAT IT TYPICALLY REFLECTS:
One or more Moderate-severity findings on specific teeth. Plaque or calculus accumulation visible at the gum line on multiple teeth. Gum recession that has progressed to visible root exposure on at least one tooth. Possibly one or two Advanced-severity findings on specific teeth, balanced by predominantly milder findings elsewhere.

WHAT TO DO NEXT:
1. Schedule a dental appointment within the next four to six weeks — not a routine recall but a visit scheduled with the screening findings in mind.
2. Bring your screening report to the appointment. The dentist can use it as a starting point for the clinical examination.
3. Review and adjust your home oral care now — brushing technique, interdental cleaning, and dietary habits.
4. Take note of any Advanced-severity findings on specific teeth. Even within a Moderate overall risk, an Advanced finding on a single tooth may warrant earlier attention.

WHAT MODERATE RISK DOES NOT MEAN:
It does not mean you have severe dental disease, will inevitably lose teeth, or need emergency dental care. Most Moderate-risk results are well managed by a planned dental visit, a professional cleaning, and adjustments to your home care routine.

MODERATE VERSUS HIGH RISK:
Both warrant a dental visit rather than waiting for a routine recall. The key difference is urgency: High risk calls for an appointment within one to two weeks; Moderate risk allows up to four to six weeks. Both require active follow-up — neither should be ignored.

WHAT IT MEANS:
A Low overall risk means the AI did not detect significant dental concerns in the photographs submitted. There may be no findings flagged at all, or only Initial-stage findings — early signs that are worth noting but do not indicate active dental problems requiring prompt attention.

A Low risk classification is the most reassuring result the screening can give. It does not, however, mean oral health is perfect or that dental care can be skipped indefinitely. It means that based on visible evidence in the photos, nothing currently rises to the level of needing prompt attention.

WHAT IT TYPICALLY REFLECTS:
No visible cavities, advanced decay, or significant gum recession. Minimal or no plaque accumulation visible at the gum line. All teeth present in the photographed views. Gums that appear pink and firm rather than red, swollen, or receded. Any findings that are flagged are typically at Initial severity — early signs addressable through routine care and good home hygiene.

WHAT TO DO NEXT:
1. Continue regular dental check-ups at the normal recall interval — every six months or as your dentist recommends. Routine recall is what keeps a Low-risk result a Low-risk result.
2. Maintain effective daily oral hygiene: brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, daily interdental cleaning, and being mindful of sugar and snacking frequency.
3. Take note of any Initial-stage findings on the report. Even though these are early-stage, they are worth understanding and monitoring.

IMPORTANT LIMITATION:
Low overall risk reflects only what the photographs show. A clinical examination is the only way to assess the full picture — including conditions that lie between teeth or below the gum line that are invisible to photographs. Low risk does not eliminate the need for in-person dental check-ups.

WHAT LOW RISK DOES NOT MEAN:
It does not mean you have no dental issues at all. It does not mean you can skip dental check-ups. It does not mean any Initial-stage findings can be safely ignored. It does not make you immune to future dental problems.

[REPORT SECTION] Potential Actions table

CONTENT:

WHAT THE POTENTIAL ACTIONS TABLE IS:
The Potential Actions section near the end of your screening report lists suggested treatments — for example, Crown, Cleaning, or Partial Denture — alongside a priority label for each. Priority is colour-coded: High in red/pink, Medium in amber, Low in blue.

PRIORITY DESCRIBES URGENCY, NOT SERIOUSNESS:
Priority describes how urgently the suggested treatment should be considered — not how serious the underlying finding is. The two are related but not the same thing. It is entirely normal to see a High overall risk report with some Low-priority treatment recommendations.

HIGH PRIORITY:
The screening has identified a condition where delaying treatment is likely to make things worse — through pain, infection risk, or further damage to the tooth or surrounding tissue. Act within days to a couple of weeks. Examples: an Advanced Cavity on a specific tooth (which may need a filling, root canal, or crown depending on extent), substantial structural damage.

MEDIUM PRIORITY:
A treatment that should be addressed within a defined window — generally weeks to a few months — but is not as time-sensitive as a High-priority item. Most Moderate-severity findings with a clear treatment pathway sit here. Schedule these actively rather than folding them into a routine recall.

LOW PRIORITY:
A treatment that should be done but does not need to be rushed. Routine professional cleaning typically appears here — even on a High overall risk report — because cleaning is part of long-term health management rather than urgent intervention. A partial denture for a long-standing missing tooth often appears as Low priority because a few more weeks of planning does not change the outcome.

WHY LOW PRIORITY CAN APPEAR ON A HIGH-RISK REPORT:
Overall risk and priority measure different things. Overall risk reflects your dental health as a whole across all findings together. Priority is per recommended treatment, reflecting how time-sensitive that specific treatment is.

If your report shows High overall risk, a High-priority Crown recommendation, and a Low-priority Cleaning, the message is: address the Crown promptly, and schedule the Cleaning when convenient — the Cleaning is still important, just not the most pressing item.

PRIORITY IS A GUIDE, NOT A CLINICAL INSTRUCTION:
The priority label is generated by the screening algorithm. A dentist's in-person assessment may agree, accelerate, or de-escalate any recommendation after examining each tooth directly. Use the priority labels to guide your conversation with the dentist, not as a fixed treatment plan.

CONTENT:

WHAT THE SEVERITY SCALE IS:
Your Oral Link screening report uses a three-band severity scale to describe how far each flagged condition has progressed on a specific tooth. The same Initial / Moderate / Advanced labels apply across all findings — plaque, gum recession, and others.

Each band is colour-coded on the dental chart at the top of your report:

INITIAL — light blue marker:
The condition has been identified at an early stage. Visible signs are present but limited. Practical impact on day-to-day comfort or function is usually minimal. Best addressed through routine dental care and improved home oral hygiene. Does not usually demand a dedicated dental visit on its own.

MODERATE — orange marker:
The condition has progressed beyond early signs. There may be visible changes you can see in the mirror, and you may notice symptoms — sensitivity, mild discomfort, or aesthetic changes — starting to appear. Typically warrants a dental review within weeks rather than months so the underlying cause can be examined and managed.

ADVANCED — red marker:
The condition is well established. Visible signs are clear, symptoms are likely, and professional dental care is needed to manage it. Generally warrants a dental review within one to two weeks. Specialist input becomes more likely depending on the specific finding.

HOW SEVERITY SHAPES YOUR RESPONSE:
Initial findings: act through routine dental care and good home hygiene — no urgent visit needed.
Moderate findings: book a dental review within weeks; this is an active schedule, not a routine recall.
Advanced findings: book within one to two weeks; the condition warrants prompt attention.

SEVERITY IS PER TOOTH, PER FINDING:
Severity is set independently for each tooth and each finding. One tooth may have Initial plaque and another may have Advanced plaque. The dental chart colours visualise the most urgent finding on each tooth. If your report flags the same condition at different severities on different teeth, the most advanced finding is what drives your overall urgency.

SEVERITY IS NOT THE SAME AS PRIORITY:
Severity describes how far a condition has progressed. Priority describes how urgently the recommended treatment should be considered. Both appear on your report and are used for different purposes.

SCREENING SEVERITY IS NOT A CLINICAL DIAGNOSIS:
A screening identifies the appearance of a condition — it is not a confirmed clinical diagnosis. Even an Advanced finding still requires a dentist's in-person examination to confirm what is happening and what treatment is appropriate.

  - How do I find which tooth my report is flagging?
  - What tooth does number 14 refer to?

CONTENT:

HOW TOOTH IDENTIFICATION WORKS ON YOUR REPORT:
Your Oral Link screening report identifies each tooth using the Universal Numbering System (UNS) — a standard scheme that numbers adult teeth from 1 to 32. Each entry in the findings table looks like:

  1st Bicuspid (Tooth 5, Upper Right)

That single line gives you three pieces of information: the tooth's anatomical name (1st Bicuspid), its UNS number (5), and which quadrant of your mouth it sits in (Upper Right).

HOW THE NUMBERS ARE ARRANGED:
Numbering starts at the back of your upper-right jaw and runs along the top arch to the back of the upper-left jaw, then drops down to the lower-left and runs back along the bottom arch to the lower-right.

Upper jaw, right to left: Tooth 1 (upper-right wisdom tooth) → Tooth 16 (upper-left wisdom tooth)
Lower jaw, left to right: Tooth 17 (lower-left wisdom tooth) → Tooth 32 (lower-right wisdom tooth)

QUICK REFERENCE — KEY ANCHOR TEETH:
Tooth 8 and Tooth 9 — the two upper front teeth (central incisors)
Tooth 24 and Tooth 25 — the two lower front teeth (central incisors)
Tooth 6 and Tooth 11 — the upper canines ("eye teeth")
Tooth 5 and Tooth 12 — the upper first bicuspids (premolars)
Tooth 14 and Tooth 15 — the upper left molars
Tooth 1, 16, 17, and 32 — the four wisdom teeth (third molars)

TOOTH NAMES AND THEIR POSITIONS:
Central incisors (Teeth 8, 9, 24, 25): the four front teeth, upper and lower
Lateral incisors (Teeth 7, 10, 23, 26): the teeth just beside the front teeth
Canines (Teeth 6, 11, 22, 27): the pointed "eye teeth"
1st Bicuspids / Premolars (Teeth 5, 12, 21, 28): just behind the canines
2nd Bicuspids / Premolars (Teeth 4, 13, 20, 29): further back from the canines
1st Molars (Teeth 3, 14, 19, 30): large back teeth, one from the centre on each side
2nd Molars (Teeth 2, 15, 18, 31): the second-from-last teeth
3rd Molars / Wisdom Teeth (Teeth 1, 16, 17, 32): the rearmost teeth

UNDERSTANDING "UPPER RIGHT" AND "LOWER LEFT":
Right and Left refer to your right and left as the patient — not the camera's view. So "Upper Right" is the right side of your mouth as you look in a mirror. However, on the dental chart in the report, which shows your mouth from the dentist's perspective, "Upper Right" appears on the left side of the diagram. This is the standard dental convention and the reason your dentist always confirms "your right side, correct?" before treating.

QUADRANT SUMMARY:
Upper Right: Teeth 1–8 (from wisdom tooth to upper front)
Upper Left: Teeth 9–16 (from upper front to wisdom tooth)
Lower Left: Teeth 17–24 (from wisdom tooth to lower front)
Lower Right: Teeth 25–32 (from lower front to wisdom tooth)

WHY YOUR DENTIST MAY USE DIFFERENT NUMBERS:
Singapore dentists often use the FDI World Dental Federation system, which uses two-digit numbers (11–48). The two systems describe the same teeth with different labels. When you bring your Oral Link report to a dental appointment, the dentist can map between the two systems without difficulty — the anatomical name in brackets (e.g. 1st Bicuspid) is consistent across both systems.

CONTENT:

WHAT IT IS:
An Advanced Cavity is a hole in a tooth caused by acid-producing bacteria that has progressed beyond the tooth surface — typically deep enough that a simple filling may not be sufficient, and prompt treatment is needed to prevent further damage or infection.

WHY THE SCREENING ONLY FLAGS ADVANCED CAVITIES (NOT SMALL ONES):
The Oral Link screening engine reports cavities under the single name "Advanced Cavity" rather than separating them into Initial, Moderate, or Advanced bands the way it does for plaque or gum recession. This is because small cavities are very difficult to identify reliably from photographs — they often start between teeth or under existing fillings where photos cannot reach. The screening flags only those cavities visible enough in photos to be confident about, which by the time they are that visible, are typically already advanced.

Important: the absence of an Advanced Cavity finding on your report does NOT mean you have no cavities. Smaller cavities are detected by clinical examination and X-rays at a dental visit.

WHAT AN ADVANCED CAVITY CAN LOOK LIKE:
A visible dark hole or pit in the tooth — often brown, grey, or black at the centre. A clearly broken or missing portion of a tooth. A larger area of darkened or softened enamel. A tooth that has already partially fractured around a decayed area.

SYMPTOMS YOU MAY HAVE NOTICED:
Sensitivity to cold, hot, or sweet foods. A dull or sharp ache that comes and goes. Food regularly catching in the affected tooth. In some cases — no symptoms at all. Advanced cavities can be surprisingly painless if they have not yet reached the nerve.

WHY ADVANCED CAVITIES MATTER:
Decay does not stop on its own. Once bacteria have breached the enamel, they progress into the softer dentine and eventually toward the pulp (the nerve and blood supply at the tooth's centre). Without treatment:
- The cavity continues to enlarge
- The remaining tooth becomes weaker and more prone to fracture
- Bacteria can reach the pulp, causing inflammation (pulpitis) and then nerve death
- An abscess can form at the root tip, causing severe pain and potentially serious complications
- Treatment options narrow as decay progresses (a filling at an early stage becomes a root canal or extraction later)

WHAT TO DO NEXT:
1. Book a dental appointment within the next one to two weeks. Available treatment options are typically broader and less invasive when acted on sooner.
2. Be prepared for X-rays. X-rays show the depth of the cavity in a way photographs cannot, and are essential for recommending the right treatment.
3. Expect a treatment plan rather than a single appointment. Depending on how deep the cavity extends, treatment may involve: a filling (if enough sound tooth structure remains); a crown (if the tooth needs more support than a filling provides); a root canal followed by a crown (if decay has reached the nerve); or in some cases an extraction (if the tooth cannot be saved).
4. Be gentle with the affected tooth until treatment. Chew on the opposite side if sensitive. Avoid biting hard foods on that tooth.

DENTAL EMERGENCY SIGNS — ACT NOW:
If you experience severe persistent toothache, facial swelling, fever, swelling affecting swallowing or breathing, or signs of a dental abscess (a pimple-like bump on the gum near the tooth, sometimes with discharge) — do not wait. Contact a dentist for same-day care or go to the nearest A&E.

WHAT THIS FINDING DOES NOT MEAN:
An Advanced Cavity finding does not automatically mean you will lose the tooth. Many advanced cavities can be successfully restored. It also does not mean every cavity in your mouth has been identified — only those visible enough from photographs.

CONTENT:

WHAT GUM RECESSION IS:
Gum recession occurs when the gum tissue around your teeth pulls back, exposing more of the tooth — and sometimes the root surface below. The clinical name is gingival recession. When your screening flags gum recession on a tooth, it means the photographs showed signs that the gum line has moved further down (or up, on lower teeth) than where it would normally sit on a healthy tooth.

Most people do not notice gum recession in its early stages because it develops slowly and without pain.

WHAT THE SCREENING LOOKS FOR:
A tooth that appears longer than the teeth next to it. A visible step or notch where the enamel meets the exposed root. Darker yellowish areas near the gum line (root surface, which is naturally darker and more yellow than enamel). A gum line that is uneven across the front of the mouth.

WHAT TYPICALLY CAUSES GUM RECESSION:
Brushing too hard or with a hard-bristled brush — one of the most common causes, especially on canine and premolar teeth where people often press hardest.
Gum disease (gingivitis or periodontitis) — long-term plaque and calculus irritates and destroys supporting tissue, causing the gums to recede.
Tooth position or bite forces — crowded or misaligned teeth can create extra stress on the gum margin.
Grinding or clenching (bruxism) — repeated heavy forces over years can contribute to recession on certain teeth.
Genetic factors — some people have naturally thinner gum tissue, which recedes more easily.
Smoking — impairs gum tissue healing and is associated with greater recession.

WHY IT MATTERS:
Once gum tissue has receded, it does not naturally grow back on its own. The exposed root surface is softer than enamel and more prone to sensitivity, decay (root caries), and further recession if the underlying cause is not corrected. Early identification — which is what the screening is designed for — gives you the best chance to slow or stop the progression.

WHAT TO DO NEXT:
The screening identifies the appearance of recession. Only a dentist can measure the recession depth, examine the surrounding tissue, and confirm the underlying cause.

The severity band on your report — Initial, Moderate, or Advanced — tells you how urgently to act. See the severity-specific chunks below for what each band means for you.

CONTENT:

WHAT IT MEANS:
Your screening has flagged Initial-stage gum recession — the earliest band on Oral Link's severity scale. The gum line shows early visual signs of pulling back, but the recession is still mild and the underlying tooth root is largely unexposed.

This is the most favourable stage to identify the problem because early recession is often stoppable at the cause level. The recession itself does not grow back — but at this stage there is usually no significant root exposure, no notable sensitivity, and no immediate functional concern. Correcting the cause is far more useful than treating the recession itself.

THE MOST IMPORTANT QUESTION AT THIS STAGE: WHY IS IT HAPPENING?
The cause is usually one of: brushing too hard or with a hard-bristled brush; plaque or calculus build-up at the gum line; or a bite or tooth position that puts extra pressure on certain teeth. Identifying and correcting the cause stops the recession from progressing further.

WHAT TO DO NEXT:
1. Schedule a routine dental check-up within the next three to six months so the cause can be assessed in person.
2. Review your brushing technique — switch to a soft-bristled brush and a gentler, angled motion (Modified Bass technique: 45-degree angle toward the gum line, small gentle circular strokes).
3. Maintain good plaque control between visits, including interdental cleaning.
4. Your dentist will measure the recession, identify the likely cause, and advise on follow-up.

WHAT INITIAL RECESSION DOES NOT MEAN:
It does not mean you need surgery, gum grafts, or specialist referral. It does not mean you have advanced gum disease. It is an early-warning marker — most useful when paired with a cause-correcting routine.

[TOPIC]      Plaque finding — what it is and why the screening flags it
[REPORT SECTION] Findings table — plaque (general)
[KEYWORDS]   plaque report, what is plaque oral link, plaque screening, plaque vs tartar calculus, plaque causes, plaque why flagged, plaque visible gum line, plaque accumulation
[URGENCY]    Depends on severity band — see severity-specific chunks
[TRIGGERS]
  - What is plaque?
  - Why does my report say I have plaque?
  - Where does plaque come from?
  - Is plaque the same as tartar?
  - Why is plaque a problem if I brush every day?
  - What does plaque look like on teeth?

CONTENT:

WHAT PLAQUE IS:
Plaque is a soft, sticky film of bacteria that builds up on teeth throughout the day. It forms naturally — every mouth contains bacteria, and these bacteria stick to the tooth surface and to each other to form plaque. When your screening report flags plaque on a tooth, it means the photos showed visible accumulation that is more than what regular daily brushing should leave behind.

Plaque starts forming within hours of brushing, which is why brushing twice a day and cleaning between teeth daily is recommended. Left undisturbed, plaque hardens within a couple of days into calculus (tartar), which can no longer be removed with a toothbrush and requires professional cleaning to clear.

PLAQUE IS NOT THE SAME AS CALCULUS (TARTAR):
Plaque is soft and can be removed by brushing and interdental cleaning. Calculus is hardened plaque that cannot be removed by brushing — only a dentist or dental hygienist can remove it with professional instruments. The longer plaque is left undisturbed, the more of it hardens into calculus.

WHAT THE SCREENING LOOKS FOR:
A pale yellowish or whitish film along the gum line. A duller, less reflective tooth surface compared to a clean tooth. Visible deposits in the gaps between teeth or where teeth meet the gum. Discoloured patches near the gum margin, particularly on the cheek-side surfaces of back teeth. In more advanced cases, signs of calculus deposits or gum inflammation.

WHY PLAQUE MATTERS:
Plaque is the root cause of the two most common dental conditions: tooth decay (cavities) and gum disease. Bacteria in plaque produce acids when they encounter sugars from food — those acids erode enamel over time. The same bacteria irritate gum tissue, causing inflammation (gingivitis), bleeding, and eventually gum recession and bone loss if left unaddressed.

Plaque is the most controllable risk factor in oral health. Unlike most dental concerns, plaque responds directly to changes you make in your daily oral care routine.

COMMON CAUSES OF PLAQUE BUILD-UP:
Brushing technique that misses certain areas — often the back teeth, inside surfaces, and the gum line. Brushing for too short a time (less than two minutes). Skipping or inconsistent cleaning between teeth. Frequent snacking or sugary drinks that give bacteria more fuel. Reduced saliva flow from medication, mouth breathing, or dehydration. Tooth crowding or dental restorations that create hard-to-clean areas.

WHAT TO DO NEXT:
The severity band on your report — Initial, Moderate, or Advanced — tells you how urgently to act. See the severity-specific chunks for what each band means for you.

WHAT IT MEANS:
Your screening has flagged Initial-stage plaque accumulation — the earliest band on Oral Link's severity scale. Visible plaque has been picked up in the photos, but the build-up is still mild and there are no obvious signs of complications such as gum inflammation or hardened calculus deposits.

This is the most favourable plaque category. The screening has surfaced early visible signs before plaque has had time to harden into calculus or cause noticeable gum problems. At this stage the situation is almost entirely under your control through changes to your daily oral care routine.

WHY IT WAS FLAGGED:
The most likely reasons are: brushing technique missing certain areas (back of the mouth, inside tooth surfaces, or right at the gum line); brushing for too short a time; skipping or inconsistent cleaning between teeth; or recent timing of the photos relative to brushing (plaque re-forms within hours).

Initial plaque does not usually cause symptoms. There may be mild gum redness or occasional bleeding when brushing or flossing, but most people notice nothing different.

WHAT TO DO NEXT:
1. Continue routine dental check-ups at your normal interval (typically every six months). Mention the screening at your next visit so the dentist or therapist can show you exactly which areas you are missing.

4. Consider an electric toothbrush. Oscillating-rotating and sonic powered brushes consistently remove more plaque than manual brushing for most people, particularly in hard-to-reach areas.

It does not mean you have gum disease, cavities, or any clinical problem requiring treatment. It does not require a special dental appointment outside of your routine recall. It is a "tune-up your home routine" signal, not a "see the dentist now" signal.

[TOPIC]      Missing tooth finding — what it means, why replacement matters, options available
[REPORT SECTION] Findings table — missing tooth
[KEYWORDS]   missing tooth report, tooth missing oral link, replace missing tooth, missing tooth options, missing molar, what happens if I don't replace tooth, partial denture implant bridge missing tooth, teeth drifting missing tooth
[URGENCY]    Planned dental visit — no immediate urgency, but earlier is better
[TRIGGERS]
  - My report says I have missing teeth — what does that mean?
  - Do I need to replace a missing tooth?
  - What are my options for a missing molar?
  - Why does the screening flag teeth that have been missing for years?
  - What happens if I don't replace a missing tooth?
  - Is it important to replace wisdom teeth that were removed?
  - How long can I leave a missing tooth without replacing it?

CONTENT:

WHAT IT MEANS:
Your screening has flagged one or more teeth as missing. Unlike most other findings, this one does not have severity bands — a tooth is either present or absent, so the screening reports it as a single state. The screening identifies a missing tooth by comparing your photograph against the expected positions of the 32 adult teeth. If a position is empty, the tooth is flagged.

WHAT THE CAUSE MAY BE:
A previous extraction (decayed, infected, or unrestorable tooth removed in the past). A tooth lost to trauma. A tooth removed for orthodontic reasons. An electively removed wisdom tooth. A tooth that was congenitally missing — never developed. The screening cannot distinguish between these histories — it can only confirm the position is empty.

WHY MISSING TEETH MATTER:
Each tooth in the mouth has a job — chewing, supporting the bite, holding the position of neighbouring teeth, and contributing to the appearance of the smile. When a tooth is missing and not replaced, several things can happen:

Adjacent teeth drift: the teeth on either side of the gap can tilt or shift into the empty space over months and years.
The opposing tooth supererupts: the tooth on the opposite jaw that used to bite against the missing tooth may grow longer because it no longer has anything to bite against.
Bite changes: as teeth shift, the way the upper and lower jaws meet can change, putting extra stress on remaining teeth or the jaw joints.
Bone resorption: the bone that used to support the missing tooth gradually shrinks because it is no longer being stimulated by chewing forces. This matters significantly if you ever want to replace the tooth with an implant — less bone means more complex surgery.
Chewing function declines: several missing teeth, especially molars, makes it harder to chew certain foods comfortably.
Speech and aesthetics: front-teeth gaps can affect speech and confidence.

These consequences develop slowly. A molar missing for many years may already have caused adjacent teeth to drift — replacing it now is more involved than it would have been at the time of loss. This is one reason the screening surfaces missing teeth even if they have been missing a long time.

REPLACEMENT OPTIONS:
The Potential Actions table on your report typically suggests one of:

Partial Denture: a removable appliance that fills in one or more missing teeth. Usually the most affordable option and a common starting point for multiple missing teeth.
Bridge: a fixed (non-removable) replacement that uses the adjacent teeth as anchors.
Implant: a fixed replacement that uses a titanium post placed in the jawbone to support a single artificial tooth or a row of teeth.

Each option has different costs, durability, requirements (such as adequate jawbone for an implant), and trade-offs.

WHAT TO DO NEXT:
1. Book a dental appointment to discuss replacement options. The dentist can assess the bone and adjacent teeth, take any X-rays needed, and outline which replacement options are suitable in your specific case.
2. Consider the time factor. The longer a tooth has been missing, the more the adjacent teeth and bone may have shifted, and the more involved any replacement becomes. Earlier conversations are easier conversations.
3. Check subsidy and Medisave eligibility. Certain procedures (such as surgical work and some restorations) may be Medisave-claimable; partial dentures may have CHAS subsidies for eligible card holders.

WHAT THIS FINDING DOES NOT MEAN:
A missing tooth does not mean replacement is mandatory. Some missing teeth — particularly wisdom teeth, or teeth removed for orthodontic spacing — do not need to be replaced. The dentist will help you decide whether replacement is appropriate, what the trade-offs of each option are, and what the cost and time commitment looks like.
